Phase 1: the cheap score at 1B

At OLMo-2-1B, the training-free compression-progress score failed its pre-registered planted-finding gate (NO-GO) and a simple word-overlap count matched it; larger scorers did better on the same items, which motivated Phase 1b.

The question#

Can a cheap score, computed without training, tell which passages in a document carry new, learnable information? Phase 1 first asked this where the answer is known: real research records with short new findings planted in them. If the score failed, the costly training-based test would not run.

What we did#

  • The score. For each passage of a record, we delete it and measure how much harder the model finds the next 768 tokens. That extra surprise, in bits (one bit is a halving of the probability the model gave the text), is the passage’s compression progress: how much more predictable it makes the text that follows. A copy mask ignores savings on words simply copied from the passage, such as a rare name repeated later, so copying earns nothing.
  • Model and dose. OLMo-2-1B, an open model: its weights and training data are public. No training: the score uses only the model’s predictions.
  • Documents and arms. 64 test items, each a segment of a real public research record. Into each we inserted one short synthetic passage stating a finding the model could not know, plus later passages that rely on it. Each finding was compared with noise (random strings, shuffled tokens, number tables) and with a same-story control: the same passage with the finding swapped for a detail that nothing later uses.
  • Tests (pre-registered, all required). T1: finding vs noise, by AUROC (the chance that a random finding outscores a random noise passage; 0.5 is chance). T2: finding vs same-story control, by win rate W (the share of items where the finding outscores its own control; ties count half). T3: the copy mask must at least halve false alarms (made-up names that recur later but get scored as high-payoff).
  • Baselines and statistics. 14 cheap alternatives, including word overlap between the planted passage and later text. A baseline “matches” if the score leads it by less than 0.05 or the interval of the difference includes 0. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over 36 clusters of related items.
  • Sealed diagnostics. OLMo-2-7B and 13B scored the same items; their outputs stayed hashed and unopened until the 1B verdict.

What we found#

  • Verdict: NO-GO, “planted gate failed (T2).”
  • T2 failed. The finding beat its same-story control in 52 of 64 items (W 0.81; the bar was 0.85).
  • T1 passed. Findings beat noise almost perfectly (AUROC 0.994). Raw surprise fails here (0.33): noise is surprising but not learnable.
  • T3 passed. False alarms on made-up names fell from 0.46 to 0.15. The whole drop was on pseudo-word names; Title-Case names stayed at 0.15.
  • Word overlap matched the score on T2’s contrast (W 0.83 against 0.81), and so did embedding similarity (0.75).
  • Larger scorers did better. On the same items, 7B and 13B met every gate criterion (W 0.875 and 0.906). Word overlap still matched the score at every size.
  • Replicated. An independent Verifier’s clean-checkout rerun on a fresh GPU reproduced all 573 score rows exactly.
  • Not run: the training-based gold standard and all later tests, as the stopping rule required.

What it means for appraisal#

  • At 1B, the score acts more like a detector of topical relatedness (“does later text talk about the same thing?”) than a measure of specific new content. It is not purely lexical: with word echoes removed, it still preferred the finding in 75% of items. (Interpretation.)
  • A score that a word count can match should not yet be used to price records.
  • Scale was the best lead. It led to Phase 1b: fresh items, a larger scorer named in advance, and a required margin over word overlap.
  • The cheap gate did its job: it stopped the study before any training spend.

Caveats#

  • Only the 1B decided. The 7B and 13B reused the same items, so they could recommend a new test but not confirm one.
  • Relatedness was balanced only on average. Within an item, the finding usually overlapped more with later text (word-overlap W 0.83).
  • Sensitivity to the finding’s exact value was not tested. The counterfactual control (the same passage with a false value) would place false claims inside copies of real authors’ records, so it was paused (D23, D28).
  • Fabrication was not tested. By design, the score measures learnable new content, not truth.
  • Cold surprise, the planned new-vs-familiar measure, did not separate post-cutoff findings from once-published ones (AUROC 0.67; needed 0.75).

Cost and date#

  • Run 2026-10-18 to 2026-10-19. The pre-registration was frozen at 2026-10-19T00:04:22Z (UTC), before any data were scored.
  • $1.93 of rented cloud GPU time (2.36 pod-hours) against a $150 cap. This includes the sealed 13B ($0.39) and 7B ($0.32) runs and the Verifier’s rerun ($0.09).
  • Measured scoring cost, GPU time only: $0.0014 per 10,000 tokens at 1B with every passage scored; $0.0047 at 13B under a sampling policy whose fidelity was not tested.
