What a report could look like

A useful appraisal says in plain words what the model now knows or does, why a buyer would care, and how the document compares with alternatives; here is an illustration for one public paper.

What a report needs to say#

James Barnes’s feedback on draft reports from the main experiment was that tables of ranks and bits did not help. A rank says a paper came 7th of 30. It does not say what the model learned, or why anyone should care. A report a person can use answers three questions in plain words:

  1. What does the model now know or do that it did not before? Name the facts, terms and methods.
  2. Why would a buyer care? Name the work where the change shows up.
  3. How does the document compare with alternatives, such as earlier work on the same topic and other documents?

Numbers back up the answers; they do not replace them. A report should also say what it cannot tell you.

An illustration: one public paper#

An illustration, not an appraisal

This example was built from main-experiment data for a public paper. The paper’s authors were not involved and have not seen it. It shows the format; it is not a verdict on the paper.

The document. “Permissive-Washing in the Open AI Supply Chain: A Large-Scale Audit of License Integrity” (arXiv 2602.08816, February 2026, about 13,600 tokens).

How it was measured. OLMo-2-13B (open) and DeepSeek-V3.1 (frontier), neither of which had seen the paper, each read it once at a matched light dose. Every result compares this with the same model after reading an unrelated paper of the same length, averaged over two runs. At this scale, a report like this costs roughly $1 per document for both models.

What the paper says#

  • Permissive licenses such as MIT, Apache-2.0 and BSD-3-Clause dominate open-source AI. They still carry conditions: include the license text, keep the copyright notice, and preserve upstream attribution.
  • The authors audit 124,278 dataset → model → application chains on Hugging Face and GitHub. They find that 96.5% of the datasets and 95.8% of the models lack the required license text.
  • Attribution is mostly lost downstream. Only 27.59% of models preserve compliant dataset notices, and only 5.75% of applications preserve compliant model notices.
  • They call this “permissive washing”: labeling an artifact free to use while omitting the documents that make the label actionable. Their conclusion is that license files and notices, “not metadata, are the source of legal truth.”

What the model now knows#

  • The open model learned the paper’s specifics. Eleven of 12 test questions improved. On average it became about twice as likely to give the paper’s answers.
  • In the open model, concrete names stuck best. The paper’s example of an MIT-labeled dataset without its license text, Anthropic/hh-rlhf, became 54 times more likely as an answer. So did the tools it names (ScanCode Toolkit 2.3 times, GitHub Code Search API 1.9 times) and its coined term “compliance payload” for license text plus notices (2.1 times).
  • The frontier model took in much less detail. Eight of 12 questions improved, and on average its answers became only 1.05 times as likely. What did register was the coined term (1.7 times) and two of the paper’s arguments: that attribution is mostly lost at “application integration” (1.6 times), and that the findings bear on legal “risk exposure” (1.6 times).
  • Broad arguments stuck less than names in both models. Single questions are noisy, with two runs each; the overall pattern is the reliable part.

How it helps with the work that followed#

  • The main follow-up is “Which Models Are Our Models Built On? Auditing Invisible Dependencies in Modern LLMs” (June 2026), by different authors. It cites the paper for the finding that license labels act as “weak metadata signals when the underlying compliance payload is missing”, reusing the paper’s own coined term.
  • After reading the paper, both models predicted that follow-up better: by 0.8% (open) and 1.0% (frontier) of its difficulty, net of the control. The average paper in the study gives about 0.4%. This paper ranks 2nd (open) and 4th (frontier) of 30.
  • The gains sit where the topics meet. The follow-up’s sentences that cite the paper improved most. Elsewhere in the follow-up, the passages that gained most were about tracing model lineage through Hugging Face metadata, and about why evidence of dependencies is scattered.
  • Part of the gain belongs to this paper alone, beyond what the other papers in its batch do for the same follow-up. On that part it ranks 10th (open) and 13th (frontier) of 30.
  • It is not shared authorship or copying. The follow-up shares no authors with the paper. Across the study, the 14 follow-ups that do share authors with their paper gained no more on average. Text copied from the paper is masked out of the scores.

Why a buyer might care#

  • For a model that will work on AI licensing, provenance or dataset due diligence, the paper supplies specific, checkable knowledge: which artifacts lack license text, which tools audit it, and where attribution breaks. It also prepares the model for the line of work that followed.
  • The effect spreads a little. The follow-ups of the four other CS/ML papers in its batch, none of which cite it, also became slightly easier to predict. On this the paper ranks 8th of 30 in the open model and 1st in the frontier model.
  • No side effects were seen on unrelated text (Wikipedia).
  • What a buyer should not infer: that the paper’s numbers are right, or what it is worth in money.

How it compares#

  • With its predecessors. Each model first read three earlier papers on the topic by other authors: “The ML Supply Chain in the Era of Software 2.0: Lessons Learned from Hugging Face”; Kim et al. (2025), “Do Not Trust Licenses You See”, which argues that license terms alone cannot settle a dataset’s legal risk; and “Model Hubs and Beyond: Analyzing Model Popularity, Performance, and Documentation”. Then it read this paper, or the control.
    • The paper’s specific content is not in those papers. With them already read, it still made its answers 2.3 times (open) and 1.4 times (frontier) more likely, more than it did without them.
    • Most of its effect on the follow-up is its own: 61% (open) and 55% (frontier) remained.
    • This matters because the follow-up cites this paper and Kim et al. in the same sentence. A buyer who already has Kim et al. still gets most of what this paper adds.
  • With the other 29 papers. It is among the strongest for its follow-up (2nd and 4th of 30), and 7th (open) and 25th (frontier) for facts absorbed. The two models disagree on how much detail stuck, although across all 30 papers their rankings agree well (rank correlation 0.75 for facts, 0.74 for follow-ups). So treat the follow-up result as the dependable one, and the detail result as model-dependent.

Where the paper sits among the 30 (open model): above average on facts absorbed (7th of 30), and second on help to its follow-up.

What this report does not tell you#

  • Whether the claims are true. In this study, a copy of a paper with its numbers and comparisons altered scores the same as the real one.
  • Price or overall worth. The report measures what models learn, relative to other papers.
  • Heavier training. The models read the paper once. More repetition raises absorption and can change the profile.
  • Independent judgment. An AI wrote the test questions from the paper; a buyer did not.

Caveats#

  • The probes were written by AI, by Claude agents working from the papers under a frozen protocol, not by buyers or independent experts. A buyer’s own questions could rank papers differently.
  • “Fact learned most” is one question’s answer, and single questions are noisy (two runs per model).
  • One light dose and two model families. Ranks also depend on which 30 papers are compared.
  • The format is a proposal. It has not been tested with buyers or authors.
