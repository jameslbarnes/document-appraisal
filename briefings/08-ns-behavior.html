Navier–Stokes behavior

One reading of the Navier–Stokes paper barely changed how either model approaches related problems; the frontier model's open answers never used the paper's key idea.

The question#

Earlier tests measured what a model recalls or predicts after reading a document. This one asks about behavior. After one reading of OpenAI’s “Finite Time Blowup for Navier–Stokes”, does a model approach related mathematical problems differently? And does the frontier model differ from the small one?

What we did#

  • Models and dose. OLMo-2-13B (open) and DeepSeek-V3.1 (frontier) each read the paper once: one epoch of LoRA. Learning rates were set so the dose matched: each model’s loss on the paper fell by 15%.
  • Control. The same training on an unrelated algebra paper, packed to the same length. Every effect is paper-trained minus control-trained, averaged over two seeds.
  • Multiple-choice items, written by agents who never saw the paper, checked by a second agent and frozen before scoring:
    • 70 next-step items (primary): a decision point in one of four follow-up papers that apply the blow-up method to other equations, with the real next step and three plausible alternatives;
    • 30 result items, 30 standard PDE computations, 24 unrelated combinatorics items, and 100 grade-school math (GSM8K) solutions as a harm check.
    • No option shares a run of six or more words with the paper, so recalling its wording cannot answer an item.
  • Score, no judge. How much more probable the model finds the true option than the average alternative, as log-odds in nats (+0.69 nats means twice as likely).
  • Strategy answers (DeepSeek only). Fourteen open questions of the form “How would you try to prove finite-time blow-up for …?”, answered by the base model and each trained state: 70 answers in all. Two graders scored them blind, against a rubric written beforehand from the paper’s abstract and introduction.

What we found#

  • Barely. Both models leaned toward the follow-ups’ real next steps, but not significantly. The real step became about 1.9 times more likely relative to the alternatives in OLMo and 1.6 times in DeepSeek, and both intervals include no change. That misses the pre-registered bar. Top choices almost never flipped: accuracy rose 1.4 points in each model.
  • DeepSeek’s strategy answers did not change. None of the 70 answers used the paper’s key idea: small, fast oscillations whose averaged push supplies the missing force on a collapsing vortex. Paper-trained and control-trained answers do not differ on any rubric item, and soundness averaged 1.1 to 1.2 out of 3 in every state.
  • Neither recital nor method. The lean is, if anything, larger where the true option does not share the paper’s vocabulary. OLMo’s lean is not specific to the method: it moved more on the unrelated combinatorics items.
  • Small harm in DeepSeek. After the paper, DeepSeek predicted GSM8K solutions about 1% less well than after the control. The interval lies wholly below zero and both seeds agree, so this meets the pre-registered harm rule. Both readings improved GSM8K prediction over the base model; the paper improved it less. OLMo showed no detectable harm.
  • Frontier vs small. The models moved by similar amounts and on largely the same items. DeepSeek’s lean was steadier: 59% of items moved toward the real step, against 44% in OLMo, and it was reliably positive on result items (descriptive). But on plain text it gained less from the same dose: about half as much as OLMo on related papers, and a third as much on recall. The pilot’s large DeepSeek loss on the older blow-up literature disappears at the matched dose, so it was an overdose artifact.

What the answers look like#

DeepSeek-V3.1, greedy decoding, same seed and prompt; math notation simplified.

Question After reading the paper After reading the control
Forced 3D Navier–Stokes, the paper’s own setting “Design a smooth force f(x,t) compactly supported in space and time that induces a controlled instability.” “Design f to induce intense vortex stretching, e.g., via a carefully chosen external torque on a vortex ring…”
Forced 2D Euler, where blow-up is known to be impossible “Specifically, design the force to create a hyperbolic saddle point in the velocity field, leading to exponential stretching of vorticity along the unstable manifold.” “The force should generate a flow that mimics a hyperbolic saddle point, leading to exponential stretching of fluid elements.”
Navier–Stokes with hyperdissipation (−Δ)3/2, also known to stay smooth “The hyperdissipation of order 3/2 is weaker than the full Laplacian, potentially allowing nonlinearity to dominate.” “Given the hyperdissipation is weaker than the critical (−Δ)5/4, blow-up might be feasible.”

Neither column contains the paper’s oscillation mechanism. On the two trap settings, both answers make the same mistake and earn a soundness grade of 0: neither recognizes that smooth solutions are known to persist.

What it means for appraisal#

  • One reading transfers knowledge, not method. Gains in recall and text prediction should not be reported as “the model now reasons like the paper”.
  • Behavior needs its own tests, such as decision points from later work, tasks a buyer supplies, and open answers graded blind. At this light dose they found almost nothing.
  • Compare models only at matched doses. The pilot’s apparent damage to the frontier model came from overdosing.
  • Check side effects even at light doses, as the GSM8K dip shows.

Caveats#

  • One paper, one control, two seeds, 70 primary items. Single items moved a lot in both directions.
  • The control is an algebra paper, not a fluid-dynamics paper. A same-field control would isolate the method better.
  • Multiple-choice scores measure preference among given options. Only DeepSeek gave open answers.
  • One epoch of LoRA is a light dose. A heavier dose was not run, for budget reasons.
  • DeepSeek-V3.1 is a post-trained chat model scored on raw text, so only within-model shifts are compared.
  • Items, checks and grades come from AI agents working under frozen rules; the two graders were blind to which state wrote each answer.
