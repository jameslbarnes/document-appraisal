Data, consent and limits

What data the study used, what it withheld, how it handled private material, and what these results do not show.

Data we used#

  • Public papers, for internal measurement only. We trained models on public papers (mostly arXiv), measured the effects, and discarded the trained weights. We do not redistribute paper texts or trained weights. Sources are recorded in manifests (ids, licenses, hashes).
  • Private material only with consent. It is processed only on GPUs we rent directly and never sent to third-party training APIs. Results for private documents are not published.
  • Contained test texts. Phase 1 used short synthetic “findings” inserted into real documents. Later tests used script-altered copies of papers. These texts stay local and are never published or quoted. The designs are described abstractly.
  • Numbers-only outputs. The repository commits numbers, not texts, test items or model answers.

An uncomfortable fact#

This study did what it studies. It trained models on other researchers’ papers without asking them. It was measurement only, with no redistribution, but the authors were not consulted. The site names papers when reporting results, as any results table would. The sample report in particular is an illustration built from experiment data; its authors were not involved. If appraisal becomes a real practice, consent should come first. That is part of the agenda.

What these results do not show#

  • Truth. The measurement cannot tell true claims from false ones.
  • Value in money. It measures what models learn, relative to other documents, not what that is worth.
  • Behavior at scale. One reading barely changes behavior. Effects at heavier training, or from many documents, are untested here.
  • Generality beyond two model families. OLMo-2-13B and DeepSeek-V3.1, both with LoRA training, which probably understates effects.
  • Independent test writing. AI agents wrote the probes and items from the documents under frozen protocols, with checkers. Buyer-written tests could read differently.

Ways appraisal could go wrong#

  • Paying for surprise. Absorption-based scores can reward surprising content, including fabrications. This is why truth checks matter.
  • Rewarding fame. Famous papers taught more facts without helping later work more. Prestige is not contribution.
  • Leaking what it appraises. Test panels can leak a private document through their averages. Outputs need limits.
  • Being used to underpay. A measurement can justify low offers as easily as fair ones. Who controls the tests and the reference models matters.

How the work was done#

AI agents (Claude) designed and ran the experiments under James Barnes’s direction: planning, code, test items, GPU and Tinker jobs, reviews and write-ups. Adversary agents critiqued the methodology. Verifier agents re-derived the numbers for the earlier phases; the later experiments still need that pass. James is responsible for the claims.
