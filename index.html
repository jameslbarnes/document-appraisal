Measuring what a document teaches a model

How to tell someone, believably, what their paper, notes or agent traces add to an AI model, what four days of experiments taught us, and a research agenda for making it real.

The question#

If you give a document to an AI model, what does the model learn from it that it did not already know? How much of that is yours rather than something it could have learned elsewhere? And can anyone check the answer without being handed the document?

This matters in two directions:

  • Contributors want credit and a say. Researchers, writers and the groups that represent them want to know what their work adds to the models built on it, and to bargain from evidence rather than guesswork.
  • Buyers want to try before they buy. A lab licensing data cannot judge its value without seeing it, and once they have seen it they no longer need to pay for it. This is Arrow’s information paradox. NDAI Agreements proposes secure enclaves as a way out. This study asks what the measurement inside the enclave should be.

The short answer so far#

Five findings

  1. Measure, don’t ask. A model’s opinion of a document’s novelty is unreliable and prone to flattery. Cheap, training-free scores failed a pre-registered test: they predicted what models actually learn only weakly. (briefing)
  2. A controlled with/without comparison works. Train one copy of a model on the document and another on a matched control document, then compare the two. It costs about $1 per document across an open and a frontier model. (method)
  3. It shows what a document teaches, and how much that helps later work. Specific content is reliably detected. Later papers that build on the document become easier for the model to predict. One reading barely changes how a model approaches problems. (main experiment, Navier–Stokes test)
  4. An open model gives the same relative picture as a frontier model once the training amount is matched. Absolute sizes differ.
  5. It cannot tell true from false. A copy of a paper with altered numbers scores the same as the real one. Measurement fixes flattery, not fabrication.

Using this on your own work#

  • Appraise a public document with the open model, using the existing code: see the runbook. It costs cents to about a dollar per document.
  • Bring your own tests. The questions you would use to decide whether a model got better at your work are the most useful extension. See W1 in the research agenda.
  • Run it confidentially, inside an enclave, with only bounded numbers coming out. See W4.
  • Reuse the agent prompts that planned, built, reviewed and ran each experiment: see agent prompts.

How the study ran#

Over four days (18–21 October 2026), AI agents ran the experiments under James Barnes’s direction. A lead session planned the work, and sub-agents built code, wrote test items and ran jobs on rented GPUs and Thinking Machines’ Tinker service. Adversary agents attacked the methodology. Verifier agents re-derived the numbers for Phases 1 and 1b; the later experiments have not had a verifier pass yet. Total compute spend was about $140.

Stage Question Answer
Phase 1 Can a cheap score spot new findings in a small model? No (NO-GO at 1B)
Phase 1b At larger scale? Yes at 13B; no at 32B, within noise
H1b Does the cheap score predict what a model learns? No
Navier–Stokes pilot Does an open model learn what a frontier model learns from one landmark paper? Yes, at a light dose
Anticipation pilot Does reading a paper help with the later work that cites it? Yes
Highlighter Can we map which passages are new to a model? Not as built
Main experiment Do these findings hold across 30 papers and two model families? Mostly; truth is not established
Navier–Stokes behavior Does one reading change how a model attacks problems? Barely

Read these as research notes

The code is research-grade, and AI agents wrote the test questions under frozen protocols. The results come from two model families (OLMo-2-13B and DeepSeek-V3.1), light training doses, and 30 papers plus one landmark case. Where a note here and a report in the repository disagree, the report is right.
