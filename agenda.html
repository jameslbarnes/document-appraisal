Research agenda

What it would take to make real appraisal possible, meaning believable, private, behavioral and affordable, organized as eight workstreams with first experiments and rough costs.

The goal#

A contributor should be able to learn what their material (a paper, notes, data, agent traces) adds to a model. A buyer should be able to learn what it would add to theirs. Neither side should have to hand over its secrets, and the answer should be believable to both.

The bar, and where we are#

Requirement What it means Status
Measured, not asked Based on what a model does after training, not on a model’s opinion Met. Cheap scores predict learning only weakly and opinions are unreliable; the with/without design works
A fair “without” The comparison model read comparable material, so the result is the document’s own contribution Met in design. The increment works; it needs better same-topic controls
Behavioral Shows what the model does differently, not only what it predicts Not yet. One reading barely changes behavior; we lack field-specific task tests
Truth-aware Does not pay for fabrication Not met. Altered copies score the same
Private Works without disclosing the document, and the tests do not leak it Designed, not built. Enclave protocol and red team only
Comparable and reproducible Same recipe, seeds and open reference models; matched doses across models Mostly met for two model families
Legible Plain-language reports a contributor or buyer can act on Started. One illustration exists
Affordable Cents to dollars per document Met for research. About $1 per document; not automated

Workstreams#

W1. Behavior and task tests#

Question. When does training on a document change what a model does, and how do we measure that for a given field?

  • What we know. One reading of a landmark Navier–Stokes paper changed predictions but not problem-solving. Knowledge sticks before methods.
  • First experiments:
    • Method from examples. Train on the paper plus several follow-ups that apply its method, then test on a held-out follow-up. Does seeing the method used repeatedly make the model use it? About $10.
    • Same-topic control. On a licensing-audit paper, ask practical scenario questions. Compare the paper against a generic licensing primer and against a competing paper, so that “anything on the topic helps” is ruled out. About $5.
    • Field task panels. Build small, frozen, probability-scored task sets per field from sources other than the document. Use standard benchmarks only as no-harm checks.
  • Open question. Should appraisals report behavior at a “realistic” single-exposure dose, at a heavier dose, or both?

W2. Truth#

Question. How can an appraisal stop rewarding fabricated or wrong claims?

  • What we know. Altered copies score the same as true ones, and false claims may even register more strongly. Later papers’ text mostly restates what a paper did, not its numbers. Matching the published record is not truth: later papers echo the original.
  • First experiments:
    • Nonsense controls. Add a word-shuffled copy and a falsified copy to every behavior test. Real content should beat both on questions with right answers. About $5–8 alongside W1.
    • Replication pairs. Collect papers whose results were later independently replicated, or failed to replicate. Test whether training on a paper that failed to replicate makes the model worse at predicting the replication. Scoping costs nothing; running it costs tens of dollars.
    • Verification of checkable claims. Agents rerun code, recheck data and re-derive math, prioritizing the claims the appraisal shows registering most strongly.
  • Design direction: living appraisal. Measure contribution at upload, then let truth credit accrue as independent work arrives.

W3. Automation and a standard recipe#

Question. Can anyone appraise a document with one command and get a comparable result?

  • Needed:
    • automatic probe and item writing with independent checkers;
    • automatic selection of controls, comparables and follow-ups;
    • per-model dose calibration tables;
    • a report schema and renderer;
    • a public reference panel for comparisons.
  • First milestone. A command that takes a public PDF and produces a plain-language report on the open model, for about $0.10–1.

W4. Confidential appraisal, in an enclave#

Question. Can the measurement run where neither party sees the other’s secrets? The buyer’s evaluation would meet the seller’s document, and only bounded numbers would leave.

  • What we know. The protocol design exists, along with a red team. Tier averages can leak a hidden test item, so outputs need budgets and panels need contributor approval. Differential privacy at these sizes costs too much accuracy.
  • First experiments:
    • port the open-model path to a confidential GPU enclave;
    • define the output budget and the attestation flow;
    • run one appraisal end to end with a buyer-supplied evaluation.
  • Natural partners. Teams that run confidential-compute infrastructure, and the authors of NDAI Agreements.

W5. Pooling, synergy and credit#

Question. Is a pooled collection, such as a co-op’s members’ material, worth more than the sum of its parts, within a field and across fields? How should credit be split?

  • First experiment. Use 3–4 bodies of material from different fields; public papers can stand in for private material. Train on each alone, then pooled. Measure:

    • within-field gains;
    • cross-field tasks, where one field’s method is applied to another field’s problem;
    • synergy beyond the sum of the parts.

    Leave-one-out runs and Shapley values give each contributor’s share. About $10–30. - What we know. Related background helped a document’s own specifics register (1.2–1.6 times). New material can also crowd out old expectations.

W6. Model coverage and dose#

  • A second frontier family, such as Kimi or Qwen on Tinker, so that “open matches frontier” does not rest on one model.
  • Full fine-tuning against LoRA. LoRA probably understates effects.
  • Dose ladders, to see where profiles diverge.
  • More fields. Biology and math papers rarely had enough recent predecessors for the increment test.

W7. Light validation against the world#

Keep this small. Check whether measured contribution lines up with signals people already trust: expert judgments, adoption, replication. A large retrospective impact study was judged overkill for now.

W8. Consent, licensing and institutions#

  • Consent first. Opt-in licensing, machine-readable opt-outs, and contracts for bulk access. A public notice alone binds little in the US.
  • A journal that asks first. Author consent before any AI licensing, with payment tied to measured contribution.
  • Co-op governance. Members approve test panels; credit and revenue follow measured contribution.
  • The “measure, don’t ask” demonstration (about $2). Show that a model’s novelty ratings shift with framing (“my idea” vs “a paper I found”), while the measurement does not.

A flagship pilot: one researcher’s unpublished body of work#

One paper changed what models know, not how they approach problems. Would a researcher’s whole unpublished corpus (notes, drafts, designs, abandoned ideas) change how a model thinks in that researcher’s fields? This pilot would exercise several workstreams at once: W1 behavior, W4 enclave, W5 pooling and W8 consent.

  • Split. Train on most of the corpus. Hold out the most recent documents and a random sample.
  • Arms:
    • the unpublished corpus;
    • the researcher’s published work only, so the increment shows what the unpublished material adds;
    • a same-size, topic-matched corpus by other researchers, to rule out “anything in this field”;
    • a word-shuffled corpus, to separate vocabulary from substance;
    • no training.
  • Tests:
    • Prediction. Does the model better predict the held-out documents?
    • Decision points. At the decision points in those documents, scored by probability with no judge, does it pick the researcher’s real choices?
    • New problems. On new problems in the researcher’s fields, do answers differ in approach, judged blind by the researcher and by colleagues?
    • No-harm. The usual side-effect checks.
  • Privacy. The corpus never leaves an enclave or GPUs the researcher controls. Trained weights are destroyed after measurement. Only bounded numbers and researcher-approved examples come out.
  • Cost. Small: roughly tens of dollars for a corpus of a few million tokens on an open model.

Near-term plan#

  1. Write the paper (arXiv) with the Navier–Stokes case as the lead, the 30-paper results as the general evidence, and truth-blindness stated plainly. A verifier and an adversary review it before posting.
  2. The “measure, don’t ask” demonstration (W8), about $2.
  3. The licensing scenario test with nonsense and same-topic controls (W1 and W2), about $5–8.
  4. Method from examples on Navier–Stokes (W1), about $10.
  5. An enclave prototype with a confidential-compute partner (W4), ideally used to run the flagship pilot on a consenting researcher’s unpublished corpus.
  6. A pooling pilot (W5), about $10–30.

How you can help#

  • Bring a document. Public, or private with consent.
  • Bring a test. The best tests are the questions you would actually use to decide whether a model got better at your work.
  • Run it on your own model and compare with our reference models.
  • Host the enclave, or review the confidential protocol.
  • Attack the method. Every result here survived adversary review, but more eyes find more holes.
