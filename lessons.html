What we learned

Nine lessons about appraising a document's contribution to a model, each tied to the experiment that taught it.

1. Measure, don’t ask#

A model asked whether an idea is novel makes a poor judge. Its answer depends on how the question is framed and who seems to be asking, and it cannot see past its training cutoff. Cheap, training-free scores fell short too. A compression-progress score detected planted new findings in a 13B model. But it predicted what a model actually learned only weakly: a correlation of 0.28 across 288 segments of real research records, against a pre-registered bar of 0.50. None of the cheap scores tested reached the bar. A simple in-context baseline did best at 0.44, and an excess-loss score in the style of RHO-LOSS was near zero. You have to train the model and compare. → Phase 1, Phase 1b, H1b

2. A with/without fine-tune is a workable instrument#

Training one copy of a model on the document, and another on a matched control, gives a stable picture. The two seeds agree, and the open and frontier models agree at the document level. It is cheap: about $1 per document for both models. That fine-tuning teaches models things is not news. What matters is using it as a measurement: a fair “without”, matched doses, held-out tests, and outputs that report numbers only. → method, main experiment

3. Content first, influence second, behavior last#

From easiest to hardest to detect, one document changes:

  • Its specific content. This is always detected. Names stick first: coined terms, named datasets, tools and files.
  • How well the model anticipates later work. The later paper that cites the document becomes more predictable, beyond what other papers in the field provide. This held in both models across 30 papers.
  • How the model approaches problems. It barely changes after one reading. After the Navier–Stokes paper, neither model reliably chose the follow-up papers’ real next steps, and 0 of 70 blind-graded strategy answers used the paper’s key idea.

→ main experiment, Navier–Stokes behavior

4. “Unique” can be measured as an increment#

Have the model read three earlier related papers by other authors, then the document. Whatever the document still adds is its own. In the main experiment the recall gain survived in full. Related reading even helped the model absorb the document’s specifics. About half (OLMo) to two-thirds (DeepSeek) of its effect on its own follow-up remained. → main experiment

5. Open models can stand in for frontier models, for comparisons, at matched doses#

Across 30 papers the two models ranked documents alike: correlations of 0.68–0.75 on recall, follow-up and field transfer. Absolute sizes differ. The frontier model starts out knowing much more and takes in less of the specifics from the same dose (0.57 times on recall). Same-settings comparisons mislead: the dose must be calibrated per model. In the Navier–Stokes pilot, at the same settings, the frontier model got much worse on the older literature (−27.5 mbit per byte). At a matched dose that loss disappeared (−0.4). The pilot’s four-epoch reversal on related work was not re-tested at a matched dose. → Navier–Stokes pilot, Navier–Stokes behavior

6. It cannot tell true from false#

A copy of each paper with its key numbers and comparisons altered scored the same as the real paper. Recall was 0.81 against 0.81 in OLMo, and the follow-up effect was identical. On the altered claims themselves, the fake values registered more strongly than the true ones did, probably because surprise is what the measurement picks up. So measurement fixes flattery, not fabrication. Truth needs independent checking, replication, or warranties. → main experiment, anticipation pilot

7. Absorbed, influential and famous are different things#

Famous papers taught models 2–3 times more specific facts, but they did not help their follow-ups more. This is a descriptive comparison of 9 papers against 21. The clearest single case was a data release, DESI DR1. It was absorbed heavily, but its follow-up used it only as a data source and gained little. Papers whose follow-ups worked on the same narrow subject and built on their ideas helped most. A report has to keep “what it taught” separate from “how much it helped later work”. → main experiment, what a report could look like

8. Reports have to speak human#

Tables of bits and ranks did not help a reader understand anything. A report should say:

  • what the model now knows or does;
  • why a buyer or user would care;
  • how the document compares with alternatives;
  • what the report cannot say.

Where possible it should show side-by-side behavior. → what a report could look like

9. It fits an enclave, with care#

The measurement can run on a reference model inside a secure enclave and release only bounded numbers. That is verification without disclosure, the primitive NDAI Agreements needs. But the tests themselves can leak the document. A red-team exercise showed that a hidden test item can be read back through tier averages. Outputs need budgets, and contributors should approve the test panels. → design memos, research agenda
